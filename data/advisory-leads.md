## rust
_Q: How to recognise coffee leaf rust and what a smallholder should do about it, including when to spray and what with._

### Recognizing and Managing Coffee Leaf Rust in Nyeri County

For smallholder arabica coffee farmers in Nyeri County, identifying and managing coffee leaf rust, a fungal disease caused by *Hemileia vastatrix*, is crucial for maintaining crop health and yield. Here are practical steps for recognition and control, based on guidance from agricultural research institutions and authorities.

*   **Recognize the symptoms early.** Look for small, yellowish powdery spots on the undersides of coffee leaves. These spots can grow larger and merge, and the upper side of the leaf may show corresponding yellow patches. Severe infections lead to premature leaf fall, which can significantly reduce the plant's ability to produce coffee cherries.

*   **Prune your coffee trees and manage shade.** Proper pruning of coffee bushes improves air circulation, which helps to create an environment less favorable for the fungus to thrive. The Coffee Research Institute, part of the Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organization (KALRO), has developed detailed guidelines on canopy management to help control coffee leaf rust.

*   **Time your fungicide sprays effectively.** For proactive management, apply fungicides before the start of the rainy seasons and continue during the early wet periods. This preventative approach is critical for controlling the disease.

*   **Use the right type of fungicide.** There are two main types of fungicides to use in combination or rotation. Copper-based fungicides, such as copper oxychloride, are used as a preventive measure, creating a protective layer on the leaves. Systemic fungicides, like those containing triazoles, are absorbed by the plant and can help to cure existing infections.

*   **Consult the Pest Control Products Board (PCPB) for approved products.** The Coffee Research Institute (CRI) conducts efficacy trials for fungicides in Kenya. Based on these trials, the PCPB registers and provides a list of approved fungicides with their recommended application rates for use in coffee. It is important to use only registered products and to follow the application instructions on the label carefully.

*   **Consider planting resistant varieties.** For long-term, sustainable management of coffee leaf rust, planting disease-resistant varieties such as Ruiru 11 and Batian is a recommended strategy. While traditional varieties like SL28 and SL34 are known for their high quality, they are more susceptible to the disease.

## cbd
_Q: How to recognise coffee berry disease (CBD) and how and when smallholders should control it._

### Recognizing and Managing Coffee Berry Disease in Nyeri County

Smallholder coffee farmers in Nyeri County can effectively manage Coffee Berry Disease (CBD), a fungal infection that can cause significant crop loss, by recognizing its early signs and implementing timely control measures. An integrated approach combining cultural practices and, where necessary, chemical controls is recommended by leading agricultural research organizations.

Here is a six-point guide for practical action:

*   **Identify Early Symptoms:** Regularly inspect your coffee trees, especially during wet and humid conditions. Look for small, dark, sunken spots on young, green coffee berries. These spots will enlarge and can cause the entire berry to rot and fall off prematurely. In damp weather, you may see pinkish masses of spores on the lesions. [CABI PlantwisePlus]
*   **Practice Good Field Sanitation:** After harvesting, remove all remaining berries from the coffee trees. Collect and destroy any fallen or diseased berries by burning or burying them to reduce the source of fungal spores for the next season. [CABI PlantwisePlus, FAO]
*   **Prune Your Coffee Trees:** After harvest, prune your coffee trees to open up the canopy. This improves air circulation, which helps to reduce humidity and creates an environment less favorable for the spread of CBD. Proper pruning also ensures that any sprays can effectively reach all parts of the tree. [CABI PlantwisePlus, FAO]
*   **Consider Resistant Varieties:** For long-term control, consider planting coffee varieties that are resistant to Coffee Berry Disease, such as Ruiru 11 or Batian. These varieties are recommended for their resilience to both CBD and coffee leaf rust. [CABI PlantwisePlus]
*   **Time Fungicide Sprays Correctly:** If you use fungicides, timing is crucial for effective control. Begin spraying before the long rains start and continue through the flowering and early berry development stages. The Coffee Research Institute (CRI) recommends a spray program that should be completed for maximum effectiveness. [CABI PlantwisePlus]
*   **Follow Recommended Spray Intervals:** The frequency of fungicide application depends on the weather. During wet weather, a spray interval of 14 days is recommended. In dry weather, the interval can be extended to 35 days. Always follow the instructions on the product label and wear protective clothing. [CABI PlantwisePlus]

## borer
_Q: How to recognise coffee berry borer and antestia bug damage and what smallholders should do._

Here is practical guidance on recognizing and managing coffee berry borer and antestia bug damage for smallholder farmers.

*   **Coffee Berry Borer:** Look for a tiny 1mm hole at the tip of both green and ripe coffee berries, sometimes with fine, sawdust-like powder. Inside an infested berry, you will find the small, black adult beetle and its white larvae tunnelling and feeding on the coffee bean, which reduces quality and yield. [CABI PlantwisePlus, FAO]
*   **Antestia Bug:** The adult bug is shield-shaped with distinct dark brown, orange, and white markings. Damage includes blackened and dead flower buds, unusual fan-shaped branching of shoots, and no visible marks on green berries; however, after drying, the beans may show "zebra" stripes and have a potato-like taste defect. [CABI PlantwisePlus, FAO]
*   **Monitor Pests Regularly:** For berry borer, start checking for infested berries 8 weeks after flowering and continue weekly; consider action if 4-6% of berries are infested. For antestia bug, scout your farm every two weeks, counting bugs on the tree in the morning or evening; action is needed if you find more than one bug per tree in wet areas. [CABI PlantwisePlus]
*   **Practice Good Field Sanitation:** After harvesting, thoroughly strip all remaining berries from the trees and collect any that have fallen to the ground. This is the most effective cultural method because it removes the places where the coffee berry borer survives and reproduces between seasons. [CABI PlantwisePlus, FAO]
*   **Prune and Manage Shade:** For antestia bug management, conduct regular and timely pruning and de-suckering of your coffee trees, as the bugs prefer to hide in dense foliage. Proper shade management can also help reduce pest populations. [Peer-reviewed studies, FAO]
*   **Use Biopesticides and Botanicals:** When the coffee berry borer is first seen entering the berry, sprays containing beneficial fungi like *Beauveria bassiana* can be effective. For antestia bugs, sprays with neem or pyrethrum have been recommended when bug numbers exceed the threshold. [CABI PlantwisePlus, FAO]

## yellow-leaves
_Q: Common causes of yellowing coffee leaves (nutrient deficiency, drought, waterlogging) and what to do, including fertiliser timing._

### Yellowing Coffee Leaves: Causes and Management for Nyeri's Smallholder Farmers

Yellowing leaves on arabica coffee plants in Nyeri County can signal a range of issues from nutrient deficiencies to water-related stress. Based on guidance from agricultural research institutions, here are practical steps for smallholder farmers to diagnose and address this common problem.

*   **Identify the Cause Before Acting:** Yellowing can be due to a lack of nutrients like nitrogen, water stress from drought or waterlogged soil, or even diseases. A soil test is the most reliable way to understand which nutrients are missing and to check the soil's acidity (pH).
*   **Manage Nitrogen for Healthy Leaves:** Nitrogen is crucial for green, healthy leaves. If a deficiency is identified, the application of nitrogenous fertilizer should be timed with the rains and based on the expected coffee yield.
*   **Ensure Proper Water Management:** Both too much and too little water can cause leaves to yellow. In periods of drought, the soil's ability to supply nutrients to the plant is reduced. Conversely, waterlogged soil can damage roots, also hindering nutrient uptake.
*   **Time Fertilizer Application with Rains:** For the central region of Kenya, which includes Nyeri, apply nitrogen-based fertilizers (CAN) during the main flowering and crop expansion periods, which typically align with the rainy seasons. Compound fertilizers (NPK) are recommended during the final expansion and maturation of the coffee cherries.
*   **Apply Fertilizers Correctly:** Spread fertilizer evenly around the drip line of the coffee tree, which is the area under the edge of the branches where the feeder roots are most active. Avoid placing fertilizer in direct contact with the tree stem.
*   **Use Foliar Feeds for Quick Correction:** To correct a nutrient deficiency quickly, especially during dry weather when the soil's nutrient supply is limited, foliar fertilizers can be sprayed directly onto the leaves. This should be done in the morning or evening when it is not hot to ensure the best absorption.

It is important to note that specific fertilizer recommendations can vary based on soil analysis and the expected yield of the coffee trees. The guidance provided in the "Coffee Farming Guide for Kenya" suggests that for late main crop areas like Nyeri, NPK fertilizers are best applied from May to July, while nitrogenous fertilizers like CAN are applied earlier in the year, from March to May, and again later from October to December.

## spray-timing
_Q: How rainfall and the short and long rains in Kenya affect when to apply copper fungicide and fertiliser on coffee._

Here is practical guidance on when to apply copper fungicide and fertilizer to your arabica coffee in Nyeri County, based on the long and short rainy seasons.

*   Apply the first round of copper-based fungicides for Leaf Rust control just before the short rains begin, and repeat three weeks later. [KALRO-Coffee Research Institute]
*   Begin the second round of copper-based fungicide sprays for Leaf Rust before the long rains start. [KALRO-Coffee Research Institute]
*   For Coffee Berry Disease, start spraying fungicides before the rains and continue until the rains and cold periods are over. [KALRO-Coffee Research Institute]
*   Apply nitrogen-rich fertilizers like CAN (Calcium Ammonium Nitrate) at the start of the long rains in March/April to support vegetative growth and repeat in May/June. [KALRO-Coffee Research Institute]
*   Apply compound fertilizers (NPK) during the long rains in May/June to support berry development. [KALRO-Coffee Research Institute]
*   A second application of nitrogenous fertilizer (CAN) is recommended at the onset of the short rains in October/November. [KALRO-Coffee Research Institute]

## harvest
_Q: When and how to pick coffee cherry for best quality (ripeness, picking only red cherry, sorting before delivery)._

For the best quality coffee, meticulous harvesting and sorting of the cherry are critical. Here is practical guidance from agricultural research organizations and publications.

*   Pick only fully ripe, bright red cherries; this ensures the highest sugar content, which is crucial for flavor and sweetness. [Peer-reviewed study, Agriculture Institute] Underripe (green) cherries lead to sourness, while overripe (dark purple or black) ones can taste fermented.

*   Employ selective picking by hand, harvesting only the ripe cherries and leaving unripe ones on the tree to mature. [Agriculture Institute, FAO] This method is considered the gold standard for producing high-quality specialty coffee.

*   Since cherries on the same tree ripen at different times, return to pick from the same trees every 8 to 10 days throughout the harvest season. [Agriculture Institute] Making several passes ensures that you harvest the maximum amount of cherry at peak ripeness.

*   After picking, sort the cherries before delivering them to the cooperative. [FAO] Remove any remaining unripe, overripe, or diseased cherries, as well as any leaves, twigs, or other debris.

*   A quick and effective sorting method is to place the cherries in clean water; dense, high-quality ripe cherries will sink, while unripe, damaged, or defective ones will float and can be easily skimmed off. [Agriculture Institute, FAO]

*   Use clean collection bags, and never reuse bags that have previously held fertilizers or other chemicals, as these can contaminate the coffee and create off-flavors. [FAO] Process the cherries on the same day they are harvested.

## quality
_Q: What lowers the quality grade and price of delivered coffee cherry and how a smallholder can avoid it._

A smallholder coffee farmer in Nyeri County can improve the quality grade and price of their delivered cherry by focusing on a few key practices at the farm level. These actions primarily involve careful harvesting, pest and disease management, and proper sorting before delivery to the cooperative factory.

Here are six practical ways to avoid lowering the quality and price of your coffee cherry:

*   **Selectively harvest only ripe, red cherries.** Avoid picking green, yellowish, or overripe (dark red/black) cherries, as these will lower the overall quality of the batch. This selective picking ensures uniformity, which is crucial for proper processing at the factory. [KALRO – Coffee Research Institute]
*   **Sort cherries before delivery.** After picking and before putting them in the bag for the cooperative, spread the cherries on a mat and remove any unripe, overripe, diseased, or insect-damaged cherries, as well as any foreign matter like leaves and twigs. [FAO]
*   **Control Coffee Berry Disease (CBD) and Coffee Leaf Rust (CLR).** These fungal diseases are major causes of quality loss. Proper pruning to ensure good air circulation and timely application of recommended fungicides can control their spread. Planting disease-resistant varieties like Ruiru 11 and Batian is a long-term solution. [KALRO – Coffee Research Institute]
*   **Manage insect pests, especially the Coffee Berry Borer.** This pest bores into the cherry, damaging the bean inside. Integrated pest management practices, including regular inspection of your coffee plants and timely intervention, can prevent significant damage to the cherries. [CABI PlantwisePlus]
*   **Ensure proper and timely delivery to the cooperative factory.** After harvesting, the coffee cherries should be delivered to the factory on the same day. Delays can lead to fermentation and spoilage of the cherries, which negatively impacts the final cup quality. [Agriculture and Food Authority Coffee Directorate]
*   **Maintain good farm hygiene.** Regularly remove and properly dispose of any fallen coffee cherries and leaves from the ground around your coffee trees. This practice helps to reduce the sources of pests and diseases on your farm, leading to healthier plants and better quality cherries. [KALRO – Coffee Research Institute]

## yield-drop
_Q: The most common reasons a smallholder's coffee yield drops from one season to the next, and how to tell which one it is._

Here are some of the most common reasons for a drop in coffee yield from one season to the next, and how to identify the cause:

*   **Pests and Diseases:** Look for specific signs of damage. Coffee Berry Disease appears as dark, sunken spots on the berries, causing them to fall off before they are ripe. Coffee Leaf Rust presents as yellow-orange powdery spots on the underside of the leaves, which can lead to the leaves falling off. Certain pests, like thrips, can cause the underside of leaves and young berries to turn grey, and the leaves may drop. [KALRO Coffee Research Institute]
*   **Poor Soil Health and Nutrition:** Observe the overall health and appearance of the coffee trees. Signs of nutrient deficiencies can include yellowing or dead patches on leaves, smaller than usual leaf size, and branches dying back after a large harvest. The most accurate way to diagnose a soil nutrition issue is through a soil test, which can identify problems like acidic soil (low pH) that prevent the coffee plant from absorbing nutrients, even when fertilizers have been applied. [Agriculture and Food Authority Coffee Directorate]
*   **Irregular Rainfall and Water Stress:** Consider the weather patterns during the growing season. A lack of sufficient water, especially during flowering and the development of the coffee cherries, can lead to a significant drop in yield. The effects of water stress can include wilting of the leaves and a reduction in the number and size of the berries. This is becoming a more common issue due to climate change, which is leading to more erratic rainfall. [FAO]
*   **Biennial Bearing:** Review your yield records from previous seasons. Arabica coffee has a natural tendency to produce a very large crop one year (an "on" year) and a much smaller crop the following year (an "off" year), regardless of the weather. In the high-yield year, the tree uses most of its energy to produce fruit, which limits its growth of new branches for the next season's crop. [Peer reviewed studies]
*   **Canopy Mismanagement:** Examine the structure of your coffee trees. If they are not pruned correctly after harvesting, they can become overgrown with old, less productive wood. This leads to reduced flowering and lower yields in the following seasons. Proper pruning helps to encourage the growth of new, healthy branches that will produce more coffee cherries. [Peer reviewed studies]
*   **Root System Pests:** If a coffee tree appears stunted, with yellowing leaves and poor growth despite adequate nutrition, the problem may be in the roots. Pests like nematodes can damage the root system, which reduces the plant's ability to take up water and nutrients from the soil, leading to a decline in yield. [CABI PlantwisePlus]

## pruning
_Q: When and how smallholders should prune or stump old coffee bushes to restore yield._

Here is practical guidance for smallholder coffee farmers in Nyeri County on when and how to prune or stump old coffee bushes to restore yield, based on available information from agricultural organizations and publications.

*   **Timing is crucial: Prune or stump your coffee bushes immediately after the main harvest.** This allows the plant to focus its energy on new growth for the next season. If you are in a very rainy area, consider applying a copper-based fungicide to the cuts to prevent disease.

*   **Stumping old, unproductive trees: Cut the main stem at a height of 30-40 centimeters (about 1-1.5 feet) from the ground.** This drastic renewal is recommended for trees that are ten years or older and have seen a significant decline in yield. Make the cut at an angle to prevent water from collecting on the stump.

*   **After stumping, select the best new shoots: Allow several new shoots (suckers) to grow initially.** After they have developed, select two to three of the most vigorous and well-spaced shoots to form the new main stems of the bush and remove the rest.

*   **Annual pruning for maintenance: Each year after harvesting, remove any dead, diseased, or unproductive branches.** Also, thin out the center of the bush to allow for better air circulation and sunlight penetration, which can help reduce the risk of diseases like Coffee Berry Disease and Coffee Leaf Rust.

*   **Manage the height and shape: To make harvesting easier and to encourage lateral growth where fruit is produced, you can cap the main stems at a height of 1.5 to 2 meters.** Regularly remove any new suckers that sprout from the main stem, as they will compete for nutrients.

*   **Systematic approach: To maintain a consistent income, avoid stumping your entire farm at once, as a stumped tree will not produce a crop for the first year.** A common practice is to rejuvenate a portion of your coffee bushes, for example, one-third, each year.

## price
_Q: How Kenyan coffee cooperatives set the payment rate for cherry, how it relates to the Nairobi Coffee Exchange, and how a farmer can check it._

A guide for smallholder arabica coffee farmers in Nyeri County on how coffee cooperatives determine cherry payment rates, the connection to the Nairobi Coffee Exchange (NCE), and methods for verifying these payments.

*   Your cooperative's payment for coffee cherry is directly linked to the prices fetched at the Nairobi Coffee Exchange (NCE) auction. The final price you receive is the auction price for your coffee, minus costs for milling, marketing, and other cooperative operations. These deductions, by law, should not exceed 20% of the sales value. [Ministry of Co-operatives, Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSME) Development]

*   To estimate the gross price per kilogram of cherry from NCE auction results, you can use a standard conversion ratio of 6.5 kilograms of cherry to one kilogram of clean coffee. For example, if a 50kg bag of clean coffee sells for KES 20,000, the price per kilogram of clean coffee is KES 400. This would equate to a gross cherry price of approximately KES 61.5 per kilogram (400 divided by 6.5), before any deductions. [Various sources]

*   The government has introduced a Direct Settlement System (DSS) to ensure farmers receive their payments promptly and transparently. Under this system, 80% of the proceeds from the sale of your coffee at the NCE is paid directly into your bank or Sacco account, while the remaining 20% is sent to your cooperative to cover costs. [Ministry of Co-operatives, Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSME) Development]

*   You can inquire at your cooperative's office for the final payment rate per kilogram of cherry for each lot of coffee delivered. Cooperatives are expected to communicate these rates to their members after the coffee has been sold and all costs have been deducted. [Co-operative Alliance of Kenya]

*   The government offers a Coffee Cherry Advance Revolving Fund which provides an advance payment to farmers per kilogram of cherry delivered. This is not the final price but an initial payment to assist with cash flow while awaiting the final payment after the coffee is sold at the NCE. [New KPCU]

*   For broader market price information, you can follow market reports from the Nairobi Coffee Exchange, which are sometimes published by agricultural news outlets. These reports can give you an indication of the prevailing prices for different grades of coffee. [Nairobi Coffee Exchange]

## inputs
_Q: Where a smallholder coffee farmer in Kenya can get approved fungicides, fertiliser and seedlings, and how to avoid fake inputs._

A smallholder coffee farmer in Nyeri County, Kenya can source approved fungicides, fertilizers, and seedlings from reputable suppliers and take steps to avoid counterfeit products. The following guidance is based on information from the Coffee Research Institute (part of KALRO), the Agriculture and Food Authority (AFA) Coffee Directorate, and CABI PlantwisePlus.

*   **Obtain certified seedlings of recommended varieties.** Look for coffee seedlings of varieties like Ruiru 11 and Batian, which are resistant to Coffee Berry Disease and Coffee Leaf Rust. The Coffee Research Institute (CRI) under KALRO operates nurseries in various locations, including Naro Moru, which is relatively close to Nyeri, and has supplied over half a million seedlings to farmers. Dedan Kimathi University of Technology in Nyeri also maintains coffee nurseries with certified seeds from the CRI. When purchasing from private nurseries, verify that they are licensed and that the seedlings are certified. [CABI PlantwisePlus, KALRO]

*   **Use recommended fungicides and check for official registration.** For the control of Coffee Berry Disease, copper-based fungicides and chlorothalonil are recommended. Specific examples mentioned include Kocide, Funguran, and Cobox 50WP. In wet conditions, a mixture of copper oxide and an organic fungicide like Dithianon may provide better results. Always purchase fungicides from licensed agro-dealers and check that the product is registered with the Pest Control Products Board (PCPB). [CABI PlantwisePlus]

*   **Apply fertilizers based on soil needs.** Coffee requires a balance of nutrients, particularly nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (NPK), as well as magnesium and calcium. A balanced NPK fertilizer, such as 22:6:13 with added Sulphur, Calcium, and Magnesium, can be beneficial for leaf growth, root development, and bean quality. Given that many coffee-growing soils in Kenya have become acidic over time, it is advisable to conduct a soil analysis to determine the specific nutrient requirements and whether lime is needed to correct the pH. [KALRO, Cropnuts]

*   **Purchase inputs from licensed and reputable agro-dealers.** To avoid fake products, always buy your fungicides and fertilizers from established and licensed agro-dealers. The Agriculture and Food Authority (AFA) licenses coffee dealers, though specific lists of local agro-dealers for farm inputs are not readily available online. It is best to check with your cooperative or local agricultural extension office for a list of reputable suppliers in the Nyeri area. [AFA]

*   **Carefully inspect packaging for signs of authenticity.** When purchasing any agricultural input, check for high-quality packaging with clear printing of the manufacturer's details, batch number, manufacturing and expiry dates, and net quantity. Ensure that the product has an intact seal. Be wary of products sold at unusually low prices or from informal vendors, as these are more likely to be counterfeit.

*   **Seek guidance from your cooperative and local extension services.** Your local coffee cooperative society is a valuable source of information on approved inputs and reliable suppliers. They often procure inputs in bulk for their members. Additionally, agricultural extension officers from the Ministry of Agriculture or KALRO can provide the latest recommendations on approved products and best practices for coffee farming in Nyeri County. [KALRO, AFA]
